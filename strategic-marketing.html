04Strategic Marketing

Position decided before promotion.

Marketing is not a list of channels. It is a decision about which readers the book is for, in what language they will recognise it, and which pathways are most likely to put it in front of them. That decision should be made before any campaign begins, not discovered halfway through one. This page shows the four decisions, and what it costs an author to leave any of them to chance.

Marketing is decided in four parts before a single advertisement is written. Each one shapes the others. Skip one and the campaign either amplifies a position that was never chosen or misses the reader the book was written for.

Position before promotion.

The work

What this service actually does.

Strategic book marketing is not the work of promoting a book. It is the work of deciding what kind of book it is, who it is for, and what it promises before any promotion begins. Most marketing fails because it starts in the wrong place: with a channel, a budget, a calendar. The correct starting place is a sheet of paper with four questions on it, and the answers to those questions determine every decision that follows.

Objective

The first decision is what success would look like, in terms a publisher or an author can actually check. Not a vague aspiration, but a number. A specific number for a specific time. A debut novel might have as its objective two hundred preorders before launch, or fifty reviews in the first sixty days, or a single organic mention in a reader community that matters. The objective shapes the answer to every other decision, because a book aiming for fifty reviews is marketed one way and a book aiming for a single mention in a specific community is marketed another.

Audience

The second decision is which readers must be reached first. Not all readers of the genre. The specific subset most likely to open the sample, finish the sample, and buy the book. This is not a demographic. It is a description of a reader in a specific situation, looking for a specific book. The description that works for that reader is different from the description that works for a broader audience, and the choice between them is the choice between a book that reaches some readers well and a book that reaches no readers at all.

Marketing is not a list of channels. It is a decision about which readers the book is for, made before any channel is opened.

Promise

The third decision is what the book promises that the books beside it do not. Not the plot, not the premise, but the specific feeling or insight the reader will have that no comparable title can give them. The promise is the spine of every piece of marketing copy that follows: the description, the tagline, the review pitch, the social caption. A book without a specific promise is a book that cannot be recommended in one sentence, and a book that cannot be recommended in one sentence does not travel.

Pathway

The fourth decision is which discovery routes reach the readers identified in the second. Amazon search, category browse, a newsletter recommendation, a reviewer with the right audience, a reader community where the promise would land. The pathway is chosen after the first three decisions, not before them, because the same book reaches different readers through different routes and only the promise tells you which route to take. Choosing a channel first is the mistake most book marketing makes, and it is why so much of it feels like noise.

  • A written strategy naming the objective, the audience, the promise and the pathway
  • The positioning shelf, showing where the book sits between its comparables
  • A realistic sequence of promotional activity built around that position
  • Notes on which channels to use and which to deliberately avoid, with reasoning
  • A recommendation on launch timing and the tradeoffs of any launch date chosen

04The cost of leaving this undone

What happens when the campaign comes before the decision.

The most common failure in book marketing is not a bad campaign. It is a good campaign aimed at a position that was never decided. The budget is spent. The posts are posted. The advertisements run. And the book still does not move, because it was never clear what the book was for, who it was for, or what it promised that other books did not. The cost is not visible in the spend. It is visible in the return.

01

The campaign amplifies an unclear position.

Advertising does not create a position. It amplifies one that already exists. When the position is unclear, advertising makes the confusion louder rather than quieter. A reader who encounters the book through an advertisement sees a cover and a line of copy that promise something vague, and they scroll past, because there is nothing specific for them to hold onto. The budget has been spent. The book has not moved. And the author concludes that marketing does not work, when the real issue is that the position was never chosen before the marketing began.

02

The wrong readers arrive, and the wrong readers leave.

When the audience is not decided, the marketing reaches whoever the algorithm decides to reach. Some of those readers will click. Almost none of them will buy, because the book is not what they were looking for, and no marketing can make it what they were looking for. The clicks look like engagement. The engagement looks like progress. But the conversion rate stays low, and the author reads the low conversion as a failure of the campaign, when it is actually a failure of the audience decision that should have come before the campaign.

03

The promise is different every time, so it never accumulates.

Without a decided promise, each piece of marketing describes the book slightly differently. The social caption promises one thing, the advertisement promises another, the newsletter blurb promises a third. A reader who encounters two of the three does not recognise them as the same book. Momentum does not accumulate, because there is nothing for it to accumulate around. The book has been marketed many times without ever being marketed once.

04

The pathway is chosen before the audience, so it reaches no one.

Most book marketing starts with a channel: Instagram, or Amazon Ads, or a newsletter sponsorship, or a BookTok campaign. The channel is chosen because it is where the author has heard books are discovered. But the channel does not decide the audience. The audience decides the channel. When the channel is chosen first, the marketing reaches whoever that channel serves, whether or not that is the reader the book was written for. The advertisement runs. The reach is real. The reader is the wrong reader, and the book does not move.

A campaign cannot fix a position that was never chosen. It can only amplify it.
